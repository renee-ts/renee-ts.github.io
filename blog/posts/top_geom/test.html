Introduction

Those who are fortunate enough to have taken a course in multivariable calculus, or vector calculus, are likely familiar with the three “derivative-like” operators: gradient, curl, and divergence. The purpose of this write-up is to observe that these three are actually all the same thing, the exterior derivative, just given a different name.

For the purposes of this write-up, I will assume the reader has seen some vector calculus, and more importantly, has some familiarity with differential forms and the exterior derivative. If the sentence, “A \(1\)-form \(\om\) on a smooth manifold \(M\) is a smooth section of \(T^*M\)” does not make sense to you, then this blog post may be difficult to follow.

Vector Calculus Revisited

Gradient

Henceforth we confine ourselves to \(\RR^3\), equipped with the standard basis. If \(f:\RR^3\to\RR\) is any smooth function (it does not matter how we define smooth, as long as it means at least \(C^1\)), then its gradient at any point \(p\) is \[\nabla f(p) = (\partial_1 f(p), \partial_2 f(p),\partial_3f(p))\] Sometimes we also write \(\grad f(p)\). This quantity is often readily interpreted as the direction in which \(f\) is increasing fastest, but note that this interpretation has already swept an assumption under the rug!

Namely, \(\nabla f(p) \in T_p\RR^3\), which is naturally isomorphic to \(\RR^3\), but not literally equal! Hence this interpretation as the direction of steepest ascent fails in the general case. Regardless, the formula as-written makes sense, and once we pick a suitable Riemannian metric, we can justify the geometric interpretation.

Curl

Now, let \(F: \RR^3\to \RR^3\) be any smooth vector field with components \(F=(F_1,F_2,F_3)\). Then its curl at any point \(p\) is given by

\[\curl F(p) = (\partial_2F_3 - \partial_3F_2, \partial_3F_1 - \partial_1F_3, \partial_1F_2 - \partial_2F_1)\]

Speaking informally, the quantity \(\curl F\cdot n\) can be interpreted as the extent to which the vector field \(F\) swirls around the vector \(n\). Notice that the curl takes in a vector field, and spits out another vector field. The output \(\curl F(p)\) can be very roughly interpreted as the direction in which \(F\) is spinning counterclockwise the fastest.

Divergence

Again, let \(F:\RR^3\to\RR^3\) be a smooth vector field with components \(F=(F_1,F_2,F_3)\). Its divergence at any given point \(p\) is given by

\[\div F(p) = \partial_1 F_1 + \partial_2 F_2 + \partial_3 F_3\]

Roughly, the divergence of \(F\) at \(p\) is the extent to which \(F\) is “emanating from \(p\)”–it is negative if \(F\) is mostly “going into” \(p.\)
